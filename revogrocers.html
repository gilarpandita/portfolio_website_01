RevoGrocers Sales Performance Analysis

RevoGrocers is a fictional grocery retailer that operates across multiple locations and sells a wide range of grocery products. Its goal is to use data to sharpen sales strategy, improve customer experience, and grow revenue. This project is a SQL case study from the RevoU Full Stack Data Analyst program. I took the role of a data analyst whose job was to find out which product categories drive sales performance and why.
The analysis uses the public Kaggle Grocery Sales Dataset, which has seven relational tables (sales, products, categories, customers, cities, countries, employees). It covers 6.76 million transactions from 98,759 customers across 452 products in 11 categories, from January 1 to May 9, 2018. The company and results are fictional and built for learning, so the findings reflect this dataset only. The brief set nine business questions:

  • • Which product category generates the highest revenue after discount?
  • • How does revenue after discount relate to total units sold in each category?
  • • How does revenue after discount relate to the number of unique customers in each category?
  • • What is the average price per unit for each category in the catalogue?
  • • How does the average price per unit relate to the number of buyers (unique customers) in each category?
  • • Which categories contribute most to overall revenue after discount, in percentage terms?
  • • Which categories have the highest repeat purchase rate?
  • • What do the results say overall about sales performance across categories?
  • • What is the cumulative transaction amount of the top customer, calculated with a window function?

I answered these in Google BigQuery, wrote up the insights with the OBIPR framework (Observation, Business Impact, Isolation, Prioritization, Recommendation), and presented the results in a slide report.
One early finding shaped the whole analysis. The TotalPrice column was 0 in all 6.76 million rows, so revenue after discount had to be derived as quantity × unit price × (1 − discount). I also cross-checked my BigQuery output against the source CSV to make sure the category totals were reliable.

What the Data Showed

Confections is the top category.
It earned about 556.9M in revenue after discount (12.85% of the total). It also leads on units sold and has the highest repeat purchase rate (99.85%).
Revenue is volume-driven
Units sold and revenue are strongly correlated (r = 0.93). Where categories break the pattern, price explains it. Produce sells more units than Snails but earns less, because its average catalogue price is lower (45.40 vs. 53.20).
Customer reach is already saturated
Unique customers per category sit within a narrow band (97,335 to 98,743, a 1.4% spread), while revenue varies almost 2x. The correlation between customers and revenue is only moderate (r = 0.62).
Higher prices go with slightly fewer buyers
Average price and unique customers are negatively correlated (r = -0.67), a mild but real price-sensitivity signal.
Revenue is diversified
Category shares range from 6.92% (Shell fish) to 12.85% (Confections). The top three (Confections, Meat, Poultry) make up 34.39% combined.
Repeat purchase rates are very high everywhere (93.68% to 99.85%)
This mostly reflects heavy purchase volume, since customers average 68 transactions each, rather than category-specific loyalty.
Grain is the consistent outlier
It has the highest catalogue price (61.40), the second-lowest revenue, and the lowest repeat rate (93.68%, while all other categories are above 97%).
The top customer (ID 94800) spent 130,324.12 across 103 transactions
That is about 3x the average customer’s total spend, driven by larger baskets (about 2x the average transaction value) more than by visit frequency (about 1.5x).

Recommendations

01/
Protect Confections
Prioritize stock reliability and shelf presence, and consider widening its assortment, since it is the largest revenue driver and the category customers return to most.
02/
Shift customer strategy from acquisition to spend per customer
Nearly every customer already buys from nearly every category. Loyalty programs, bundles, and replenishment nudges will do more than broad acquisition campaigns.
03/
Run a targeted test in Grain
Try a modest price test or an assortment review to find out whether its weak performance comes from price sensitivity or simply lower demand.
04/
Keep investment balanced across the portfolio
Revenue is diversified, so give the top three categories a modest tilt in promotions without neglecting the other eight.
05/
Use the top customer’s profile to define a high-value segment
The pattern is larger baskets with moderately higher frequency. Retention offers for this segment should focus on growing basket value.

What I delivered

Problem framing
Turned nine business questions into a structured category-level analysis of sales performance.
Data exploration and validation
Checked 6.76M sales rows for duplicates, missing values, and broken keys. Found that TotalPrice was empty and derived revenue after discount from quantity, unit price, and discount.
BigQuery SQL
Wrote the queries for revenue, units sold, unique customers, catalogue pricing, revenue contribution, repeat purchase rate, and the top customer’s cumulative spend using a window function.
Descriptive statistics
Calculated count, min, max, range, mean, median, mode, Q1, Q3, variance, standard deviation and skewness for Quantity, Discount and Total Revenue, each with a business interpretation.
Relationship analysis
Compared revenue against volume, customer reach, and price at the category level to separate what actually drives revenue.
OBIPR recommendations
Consolidated the findings into one Observation, Business Impact, Isolation, Prioritization, and Recommendation narrative.
Slide report
Built a presentation covering the business overview, methodology, a slide per question (SQL, query output, insight), and a recap with business recommendations.
